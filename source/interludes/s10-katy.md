## K1. The cave

You have stepped a little way off from the others. The ukulele is across your knees. Behind you, voices are low, your father's among them, and you don't try to hear. The green light has gone out of the walls. Ash sits on everything: on your boots, in the creases of your knuckles, on the bronze of the ukulele where your thumb has worn it bright.

Your fingers find the four notes on their own. A, D, F, E. The start of the song he wrote. The song she sang.

You have played it a hundred times since you left court. You have never once played it slowly enough to hear her in it.

You do now. The cave goes quiet around the notes. Then it goes somewhere else.

## K2. Under the eaves

You are seven, and you are in bed, and you are not asleep.

The room is the narrow one at the top of the servants' wing in Valethorn, where the ceiling slopes so low that your mother has to duck by the window. A candle stub burns on the sill in a dish of its own wax. Her shortbow hangs on its peg by the door, unstrung, the way it always hangs. The pillow smells of her perfume, because it is her pillow too.

Outside, the palace is still awake. Far off, someone laughs a high, careful court laugh. A door closes. The stone holds the cold of the evening, and you have pulled the blanket up to your nose.

You have been waiting a long time. You are good at waiting. You are better at pretending.

## K2b. The waiting game

You have a game for waiting. You name everything in the room in your head, in order, and if you get to the end before she comes, you start again in Elvish.

Candle. Sill. Bow. Peg. Door. Latch. The crack in the plaster shaped like a running dog. Her other dress, on its hook. Her hairbrush. The bow again, because it's your favourite and you're allowed to count it twice.

Then the sounds, because sounds count too. The wind in the gutter. Someone's cart down in the yard. The palace, breathing.

You are on your third time round, in Elvish, slower now, the words getting further apart, when you hear the stair.

→ Continue (go to K3)

## K3. Her footsteps

You know her footsteps before you know anything else. The servants' stair has eleven steps and the ninth one creaks, and she always steps over it, except when she is tired.

Tonight the ninth step creaks.

The latch lifts. The candle flame leans toward the door and straightens. Her perfume reaches you before she does. She smells of the long corridors too: polish, cold stone, other people's candles.

She sits on the edge of the bed, and the mattress tips you toward her. She puts the back of her hand on your cheek to see if you are warm. Her hand is cold as a spoon.

Somewhere inside the memory, the older you holds very still. You had forgotten the cold hands.

How does little Katy greet her?

## K4a. "Sing it again."

You give up. You open your eyes and say it into the blanket, so it comes out muffled. "Sing it again."

"Again?" She laughs, very softly, the laugh she keeps for this room. "I haven't sung it once yet."

"Sing it again anyway."

"You were never asleep, were you, little bard." She tucks the blanket under your chin, tight, the way you like it, so you are a parcel. Her fingers smell of candle smoke. "Was I very late?"

You nod. She nods back, as serious as you.

"Then I owe you a song. One. And then you close your eyes for real, and I check."

You know she won't check. You know she'll sing it twice.

→ Continue (go to K5)

## K4b. Pretending

You keep your eyes shut and your breathing slow, in and out, the way you practised.

She believes you, or she lets you think she does. She sits a while without moving. Her shoes come off, one, two. She lets out a breath that is longer than a breath.

Then she finds your hand on top of the blanket. Your fingers are tapping. You didn't know they were.

"You have his hands, you know," she says, very quietly, to someone who is asleep. "Always drumming."

She holds them still. Not to stop them. Just to hold them.

The older you, inside the memory, wants to open her eyes and ask whose. The child doesn't. The child is asleep.

→ Continue (go to K5)

## K4c. "Where's my father?"

"Where's my father?"

You don't plan to ask it. It's just the thing in your mouth, the way it is some nights.

She goes still. The candle gets a long breath to itself. You count it, because you count things: one, two, three, four.

"He was a bard," she says at last. "Very bad at staying. Very good at songs."

"Is that all?"

"That's most of it." She brushes the hair off your forehead. Her hand is cold, so she warms it against her own neck and tries again. "The rest is too long for tonight."

She says nothing else. She sings instead, as if the song is the rest of the answer, and this is the only shape it fits.

→ Continue (go to K5)

## K5. The song

She sings low, so the song stays in the room and doesn't go out under the door.

*Foxglove bells over the sleeping roots,*
*ring soft, and low, and slow for me;*
*the road runs far, but the road runs home,*
*and I'll be back before the snow.*

The child knows every word. The child sings the last line with her, a beat late, mouth half in the blanket.

The older you knows something the child doesn't. You learned it today, in a cave, from a grey-armed man with a lute. He wrote this, the summer they met. Every word your mother is singing to you, he gave her first.

The edges of the room have gone thin, like paper held up to a lamp. You could step out of the memory if you wanted to. You don't want to.

→ Continue (go to K6)

## K6. The hum

The verse ends, and she doesn't stop.

She goes round the tune again with no words, humming, mouth closed, slower than before. It is the same melody, but it waits on notes the first verse hurried past. The child has heard this part on nights when her mother thought she was asleep. The child never learned any words for it. She always thought there weren't any.

You are not so sure, now.

Your mother's hand is on your hair. The candle is down to a coin. The palace has gone quiet at last, and in the quiet her hum is the only thing moving.

What do you do?

## K7a. The words

You know the words. You didn't, at seven. You do now. Your father sang them in a cave today, and you sang them back to him. [SAM?: only if Katy's player sang or heard verse two at the table. If not, this passage has her hum along instead of singing, and K8a has Elodie sing the words to her.]

So you sing, inside the memory, in the child's voice.

*Foxglove bells over the sleeping roots,*
*hush now, my heart, and do not weep…*

Your mother stops.

She takes her hand off your hair. In the last of the candle her face is very close to yours, and it isn't the face she wears for the princess's wing. It is younger than that, and frightened, and something else.

"Who taught you that?" she whispers. "I never…"

You don't have an answer a seven-year-old could give. You sing the next line instead.

→ Continue (go to K8a)

## K8a. Two voices

*and if you can't find me, sing this song,*

For a moment she only listens. Then she smiles, the slow kind that starts in her eyes and takes its time reaching her mouth, and she sings the last line with you.

*and I'll come find you in your sleep.*

Two voices in a room under the eaves. Hers is surer. Yours is seven years old and slightly flat, and she doesn't correct it.

When it's done she doesn't ask again who taught you. She looks at you as if you have brought her something from very far away and she doesn't want to know how, in case knowing breaks it. She puts her cold hand flat on your chest and leaves it there.

"Well," she says, and her voice isn't steady. "Then you know it too."

→ Continue (go to K9)

## K7b. Listening

You lie still. You let your breathing go slow, and this time you aren't pretending, not quite. The hum is warm and the blanket is warm and the child is halfway under.

Your mother thinks you've gone. You can tell, because her voice changes. It stops being careful. She is singing for herself now.

The hum opens into words.

Not his words. You know his now. These are different, and they are hers. You can hear it in how she sings them, testing each line before she trusts it, the way you do when a song is new and you're not sure it's any good.

→ Continue (go to K8b)

## K8b. Her own verse

*Foxglove bells over the sleeping roots,*
*my little one, the night is long;*
*and should I go where the bells don't ring,*
*you'll find me waiting in the song.*

[SAM?: Elodie's own third verse, new canon. Safer last line if this feels like a promise: "I'll hum you home the whole way long."]

She sings it twice. The second time she changes nothing. She has decided.

The child is asleep. The child won't remember this, and didn't, for nineteen years.

The older you holds every word the way you'd carry water in cupped hands. Nobody else has heard this verse. It was never written down.

Your mother bends, kisses your forehead, and says to nobody, "That one's just ours."

→ Continue (go to K9)

## K7c. The question

"What are the words?" you ask. "For that part."

She stops humming. "Which part?"

"The part with no words."

"Ah." She taps your nose with one cold finger. "Those words are for when you're older."

"How much older?"

"Older." She thinks about it, then sits up straighter, the way she does before she teaches you a dance step. "I'll give you something else instead. Something for court. Give me your hands."

You put them out from under the blanket. She takes them, turns them over, and studies them as if they were a letter.

→ Continue (go to K8c)

## K8c. Court eyes

"When the ladies smile at you," she says, "don't look at their smiles. Anyone can be taught to smile. Look at their hands."

She shows you. She makes her face pleasant, the face for the princess's wing, and at the same time she picks at a thread on her cuff, over and over, small tight pulls.

"What does that say?"

"That you want to go," you say.

"Good." She stops pulling. "Look at their hands, not their smiles. Hands can't lie as well as faces." She folds your fingers closed, one at a time. "And keep yours still when you need to. Yours drum."

The child thinks it's a game. The older you used it at court every day for the next fourteen years.

→ Continue (go to K9)

## K9. The bell

Then the bell.

It is a small silver bell, rung once, somewhere down the passage. Not loud. It doesn't need to be. Everyone in the servants' wing knows the bell from the princess's wing.

Your mother stiffens. You feel it through the mattress before you see it. Her hand stops. Her face changes one piece at a time into the other face, the one with no tiredness in it.

"Already," she says under her breath. Not to you.

She doesn't stand yet. She looks at the door, then at you, and for a moment she is two people in one body, and both of them are hers.

What does little Katy do?

→ Hold on to her hand. (go to K10a)
→ Let her go. (go to K10b)

## K10a. Holding on

You take her hand before she can stand, and you hold on with both of yours.

She could pull free. She doesn't. She stays half turned to the door and gives you one more line, the last one, so quietly it is mostly breath: *and I'll be back before the snow.*

The bell rings again. Down the passage, someone's slippers are already hurrying.

She lifts your knuckles to her mouth and kisses them, all four, one after another. "I'll be back before the snow," she says, and this time it isn't the song. It's a promise, and she says it like one.

Then she takes her hand back, gently, and goes. The ninth step creaks. The door doesn't quite close.

→ Continue (go to K11)

## K10b. Letting go

You let her go. You are seven and you already know how this works: the bell rings, she goes, she comes back.

She stands. At the door she stops, comes back, and kneels by the bed. She takes the little bottle from her sleeve, the one she isn't supposed to waste, and touches the stopper to the inside of your wrist. Cold, then warm. The smell of her, on you.

"So you can find me in the dark," she says.

Then she is the other face again, and she is gone, and the ninth step creaks.

The room smells of her for a long time after. You fall asleep with your wrist against your mouth.

→ Continue (go to K11)

## K11. Back

The memory folds up like a letter.

There's no moment when it ends. The candle is there, and then the candle is the last green light dying in a crack in the cave wall. The blanket is the weight of the ukulele across your knees. Your fingers have stopped playing. You don't know when.

Ash on your boots. The dead Heart ticking as it cools. Voices behind you, still low. Thorn, a shape at the edge of the lamplight, carefully not looking at you.

Nobody saw you go anywhere. You didn't go anywhere. You are twenty-six, sitting on a rock in a cave in Summerwood, and your face is wet, and you don't wipe it.

→ Continue (go to K11b)

## K11b. Her hands, your hands

You look down at your hands. They're bigger than the ones she held. The fingertips are hard from years of strings, the nails short and not very clean. Ash in every line of the palms.

They're drumming. Very slightly, on the ukulele's body, without your asking them to.

You let them. For once you don't make them stop.

Behind you, the voices are still low. Nobody has noticed that you stopped playing. You're glad. You'll start again in a moment. You just want to sit here first, with the cold of the cave on your face and the last of a room under the eaves still fading out of your nose.

## End A. Two Voices

You play the four notes again, and this time you don't stop after the first verse. You play the second one through, under your breath, the way you just sang it with her.

She knew it. She stopped and stared, and then she knew it. Somebody brought those words to Valethorn long before you ever heard them, and she kept them, and never once sang them to you while you were awake.

You put your hand flat on the ukulele's strings to quiet them, the way she put hers on your chest.

## End B. Mother's Hum

You don't play anything. You sit with your hands on the strings and say the four lines over in your head, once, then again, the way you'd check a lock.

Your mother's verse. It was never written down, and the one person who knew it is dead. Now two people know it, and one of them is you.

Later you'll write it down. Not yet. For now it's still warm.

You look up, across the cave, at the people you came here with. Not at their faces. At their hands.
