## K1. The cave

You've moved a little way off from the others and sat down with the ukulele across your knees. They're talking low behind you, your father among them, and you're not listening.

The green light has gone out of the walls. There's ash on your boots and in the creases of your knuckles, and on the ukulele, all but the patch of bronze your thumb has rubbed bright.

Your fingers go to the four notes without being asked. A, D, F, E, the start of the lullaby. He wrote it and she sang it.

You've played it a hundred times since you left court, always too quick. Tonight you play it at her speed, and it sounds like her.

The cave goes quiet. Then there's no cave, and you can smell a candle.

## K2. Under the eaves

You are seven and you're supposed to be asleep.

It's the narrow room at the top of the servants' wing in Valethorn, where the ceiling slopes so low that your mother has to duck at the window. A candle stub burns on the sill in a dish of its own wax. Her shortbow hangs unstrung on its peg by the door, where it always hangs. The pillow is hers too, so it smells of her perfume.

The palace is still up. Far off someone laughs, high and careful, a court laugh. A door closes.

You're good at waiting and better at playing asleep. The stone keeps the cold, so the blanket is up to your nose.

## K2b. The waiting game

Your game for waiting is to name everything in the room, in order, in your head. If you reach the end and she still hasn't come, you start again in Elvish.

Candle, sill, bow, peg, door, latch. The crack in the plaster that looks like a running dog. Her other dress on its hook, then her hairbrush. Then the bow again, because it's your favourite and you've decided it counts twice.

Sounds count too. The wind in the gutter. Someone's cart down in the yard. The palace, breathing.

You're on your third time round, in Elvish by now, with the words coming slower and further apart, when you hear the stair.

→ Continue (go to K3)

## K3. Her footsteps

You'd know her footsteps anywhere. The servants' stair has eleven steps and the ninth one creaks. She steps over it, unless she's tired. Tonight it creaks.

The latch lifts. The candle flame leans toward the door and stands up again. Her perfume reaches you before she does, and under it floor polish and other people's candles.

She sits on the edge of the bed and the mattress tips you toward her. She lays the back of her hand on your cheek to see if you're warm. Her hand is as cold as a spoon.

The grown you, inside the child, keeps very still. You had forgotten her cold hands.

## K4a. "Sing it again."

You give up. You open your eyes and say it into the blanket, so it comes out mostly wool. "Sing it again."

"Again?" She laughs under her breath, the laugh you only hear in this room. "I haven't sung it once yet."

"Sing it again anyway."

"You weren't asleep at all, were you, little bard." She tucks the blanket under your chin, good and tight, how you like it, until you're a parcel. Her fingers smell of candle smoke. "Was I very late?"

You nod. She nods back and doesn't smile.

"Then I owe you a song. One. After that you shut your eyes properly, and I check."

She has never once checked. Most nights you get the song twice.

→ Continue (go to K5)

## K4b. Pretending

You keep your eyes shut and breathe slowly, in and out, as practised.

She believes it, or is kind enough to act as if she does. For a while she just sits. Her shoes come off, first one and then the other. She lets out a breath you could sing a whole line on.

Then she finds your hand on top of the blanket. Your fingers are tapping, and you hadn't noticed.

"You have his hands, you know," she says quietly, to a child who's asleep. "Always drumming."

She closes her hand over your fingers and lets them go on tapping inside it.

The grown you wants to open your eyes and ask whose hands. The seven-year-old is busy being asleep, so nobody asks.

→ Continue (go to K5)

## K4c. "Where's my father?"

"Where's my father?"

You hadn't planned to ask. Some nights the question is sitting in your mouth, and tonight it got out.

She goes still. The candle gets a long breath of draught from under the door, and you count while she says nothing. One, two, three, four.

"He was a bard," she says. "Very bad at staying. Very good at songs."

"Is that all?"

"That's most of it." She brushes the hair off your forehead. Her hand is cold, so she warms it on her own neck and tries again. "The rest is too long for tonight."

She says nothing else and starts the song.

→ Continue (go to K5)

## K5. The song

She sings low, so the song stays in the room and none of it gets out under the door.

*Foxglove bells over the sleeping roots,*
*ring soft, and low, and slow for me;*
*the road runs far, but the road runs home,*
*and I'll be back before the snow.*

The child knows every word. She comes in on the last line a beat late, with half her mouth in the blanket.

You learned today, in a cave, from a man with a lute and arms grey to the elbow, that he wrote this the summer he met her. Every word your mother is singing, she had from him.

The edges of the room have gone thin, and the cave's lamplight shows through the plaster. You could walk out of the memory now. You stay where you are.

→ Continue (go to K6)

## K6. The hum

The verse ends and she keeps going.

She takes the tune round again with her mouth closed, slower, holding notes that the verse hurried through. The child has heard this part before, on nights when her mother thought she'd dropped off. Nobody taught her words for it. She decided long ago that it had none.

After today you'd bet money that it does.

Her hand is on your hair. The candle is down to a puddle the size of a coin. The palace has gone to bed at last, and her humming is the only sound on the top floor.

## K7a. The words

You know the words. You didn't at seven, but your father sang them in a cave today and you sang them back. [SAM?: only if Katy's player sang or heard verse two at the table. If not, this passage has her hum along instead of singing, and K8a has Elodie sing the words to her.]

So you sing them, inside the memory, in the child's voice.

*Foxglove bells over the sleeping roots,*
*hush now, my heart, and do not weep…*

Your mother stops humming. She takes her hand off your hair. There's enough candle left to see her face, close to yours. She's left off the face she wears in the princess's wing. This one is younger, and scared.

"Who taught you that?" she says, barely aloud. "I never…"

No answer you have would make sense from a seven-year-old, so you sing the next line.

→ Continue (go to K8a)

## K8a. Two voices

*and if you can't find me, sing this song,*

She listens to the whole of that line. A smile starts round her eyes and takes its time getting to her mouth, and then she sings the last one with you.

*and I'll come find you in your sleep.*

Two voices in a room under the eaves, and hers is the sure one. Yours is seven years old and a shade flat, and she doesn't correct it.

She doesn't ask again who taught you. She looks at you for a long time, then lays her cold hand flat on your chest and leaves it there.

"Well," she says, and it wobbles. "Then you know it too."

→ Continue (go to K9)

## K7b. Listening

You lie still and let your breathing slow. This time most of it is real. The blanket is warm and so is the hum, and the child is halfway under.

Your mother thinks you've gone, and her voice stops being careful. She's singing for herself.

Then the hum has words in it, and they're new to you. You know your father's verses by now and these are different. She tries each line under her breath before she lets it out. You do exactly that with a song you've only just made and don't trust yet. She wrote these herself.

→ Continue (go to K8b)

## K8b. Her own verse

*Foxglove bells over the sleeping roots,*
*my little one, the night is long;*
*and should I go where the bells don't ring,*
*you'll find me waiting in the song.*

[SAM?: Elodie's own third verse, new canon. Safer last line if this feels like a promise: "I'll hum you home the whole way long."]

She sings it through twice. The second time she doesn't change a word, so it's finished.

The child is asleep. She won't remember this in the morning, and she didn't for nineteen years.

You get every word by heart as it goes past. She never wrote this verse down and nobody else has heard it.

Your mother bends and kisses your forehead. "That one's just ours," she says, to nobody.

→ Continue (go to K9)

## K7c. The question

"What are the words?" you ask. "For that part."

She stops humming. "Which part?"

"The part with no words."

"Ah." She taps your nose with one cold finger. "Those are for when you're older."

"How much older?"

"Older." She thinks for a bit. Then she sits up straighter, which usually means a dance step is coming. "I'll give you something else instead. Something for court. Give me your hands."

You get them out from under the blanket. She takes them, turns them palm up and has a good long look.

→ Continue (go to K8c)

## K8c. Court eyes

"When the ladies smile at you," she says, "don't look at the smile. Anyone can be taught to smile. Look at their hands."

She shows you. She puts on her pleasant face, the one for the princess's wing, and while it stays pleasant she picks at a thread on her cuff. Small, tight pulls, again and again.

"What does that say?"

"That you want to go," you say.

"Good." Her fingers stop. "Hands, not smiles. Hands can't lie as well as faces." She folds your fingers shut one at a time. "And keep yours still when you need to. Yours drum."

At seven you think it's a game. You go on to play it at court every day for the next fourteen years.

→ Continue (go to K9)

## K9. The bell

A bell rings once, down the passage. It's a small silver one and it isn't loud, but everybody in the servants' wing knows the bell from the princess's wing.

Your mother goes stiff. You feel it through the mattress before you see it. Her hand stops where it is. Then her face changes over, a piece at a time, to her other one. That one never looks tired.

"Already," she says under her breath. She isn't saying it to you.

She doesn't get up yet. She looks at the door, then at you, then at the door.

## K10a. Holding on

You grab her hand before she can stand and hang on with both of yours.

She could pull free without trying. She stays, half turned to the door, and gives you one more line, the last one, mostly breath. *and I'll be back before the snow.*

The bell rings again. Down the passage somebody's slippers are already hurrying.

She lifts your hand and kisses the knuckles, all four, one after the other. "I'll be back before the snow," she says. There's no tune in it this time, and she's looking straight at you.

Then she takes her hand back, gently, and goes. The ninth step creaks. The door doesn't quite close.

→ Continue (go to K11)

## K10b. Letting go

You let her go. You're seven and you know how the bell works. It rings and she goes, and later she comes back.

She stands and gets as far as the door. Then she comes back, and kneels by the bed. She has a little bottle up her sleeve, the perfume she isn't supposed to waste. She touches the stopper to the inside of your wrist. It's cold, then warm, and now you smell of her.

"So you can find me in the dark," she says.

She puts her other face on, and then she is gone. The ninth step creaks.

The room smells of her for a long time. You fall asleep with your wrist against your mouth.

→ Continue (go to K11)

## K11. Back

You can't find the join where it ends. The candle on the sill turns out to be the last green light, going out in a crack in the cave wall. The blanket turns out to be the ukulele across your knees. Your fingers have stopped playing and you couldn't say when.

There's ash on your boots. The dead Heart ticking as it cools can't keep time. Voices behind you, still low. Thorn stands at the edge of the lamplight, taking great care to look somewhere else.

Nobody saw you go anywhere, because you didn't. You're twenty-six, sitting on a rock in a cave in Summerwood. Your face is wet. You leave it.

→ Continue (go to K11b)

## K11b. Her hands, your hands

You look at your hands. They're a good deal bigger than the pair she held. The fingertips are hard from years of strings, the nails short and none too clean. There's ash in every line of both palms.

They're drumming, a light patter on the body of the ukulele that you didn't ask for. Mostly you stop them when you catch them. This time you don't.

Nobody behind you has noticed that the playing stopped, which suits you. You'll start again in a minute, once the smell of that attic room has gone out of your nose.

## End A. Two Voices

You play the four notes again, and this time you keep going after the first verse. You play the second one too, singing it under your breath as you sang it with her.

She knew it. She stopped and stared, and then she came in on the last line without having to think. So those words got to Valethorn long before you heard them. She kept them, and never once sang them to you while you were awake.

You lay your hand flat on the strings, as she laid hers on your chest, and the last note stops.

## End B. Mother's Hum

You don't play. You sit with your hands resting on the strings and go through her four lines in your head. Then you go through them again, to be sure they're all still there.

It's your mother's verse. She never wrote it down. Since she died nobody alive has known it, and now you do.

You'll put it on paper later. Tonight you just say it over once more.

You look up, across the cave, at the people you came here with. You skip the faces and go to the hands.
